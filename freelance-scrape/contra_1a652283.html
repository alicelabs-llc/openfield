Costumes Parisiens 2026: George Barbier's Paris fashion plates of 1913, dressed for the way we live now. In 1913 George Barbier drew the latest Paris fashion for the Journal des Dames et des Modes: flat stencil colour, one elegant figure per plate, and a caption announcing the look, like "Robe d'intérieur" or "Manteau de théâtre". I found his Costumes Parisiens plates at the Rijksmuseum, where they are in the public domain, cropped ten of them down to the bare stencil and built a Recraft V4 Style from them. Costumes Parisiens 2026 keeps his poses and his captions and dresses them for how we actually live now: a silk blouse over pyjama trousers for the video call, a red delivery backpack worn over a fur-trimmed coat, a theatre coat embroidered with 99 unread badges, a necklace of charging cables, earbuds worn as pearl drops, a ring light carried like a parasol. Same magazine, a hundred and thirteen years later; only the accessories have changed.

The New Book of Beasts A Medieval Bestiary of Modern Human Nature What if medieval bestiaries described the habits of modern people instead of mythical creatures? For the Recraft × Contra Styles Challenge, I created Illuminated Bestiary, a custom Recraft Style inspired by public-domain illustrations from 13th–15th-century manuscripts. I used historical references from the Public Domain Image Archive to explore medieval animal anatomy, ornamental details, ink drawings, and muted natural pigments. Then I applied the style to eight imaginary beasts, each representing a familiar modern behavior: overthinking, people-pleasing, doomscrolling, procrastination, ghosting, perfectionism, attention-seeking, and impostor syndrome. The result is a collection of creatures that look like they belong in a centuries-old manuscript, even though their habits are very much our own. What if a botanical brand functioned as an archive rather than a catalogue? FLORAE is a speculative identity exploring the relationship between nature, observation, and time. Imagined as a living botanical archive, the brand documents the small, often overlooked changes through which growth reveals itself. Each specimen becomes a record. Each observation adds to a growing collection. Together, they form an evolving archive of botanical forms, patterns, and transformations. The visual identity draws from scientific documentation, editorial typography, organic textures, and a restrained botanical palette. Numbered observations, specimen records, and archival language establish a visual system that feels both analytical and alive, balancing the precision of documentation with the unpredictability of nature. Rather than presenting nature as something static to be collected, FLORAE treats it as something to be observed, interpreted, and continuously rediscovered.
